Story Network Plan

How Versatile should turn a novel into a story network that keeps growing as the book is written, keeps its history, and can be read by volume, chapter, scene and moment in time. Version 2 is built on a review of about 60 papers and tools. It replaces v1's design where the research disagreed with it, and turns the result into small steps, each with a test that has to pass before the next one starts.

§0The plan in one sentence

First find the cast and let code propose every mention, then let the small model only choose (which character, which relation, duplicate or not), keep every fact as a quoted, dated claim on two clocks, let code decide what changes, and measure each step against a hand-labelled gold set before building the next.

§0.5Words used in this plan

Cast
The list of characters (and important places and objects) in a book, each with an id, a main name and its aliases.
Mention
One place in the text that refers to something: "Zeena", "Mrs. Frome", "the sick woman", "she".
Linking
Deciding which cast member a mention points to, or "none". This is the cheap, practical form of coreference.
Claim
One small fact the text states, with the quote that proves it, the scene it comes from, and when it is true.
Modality
Whether a claim really happened in the story ("asserted") or is negated, hoped, imagined, planned, or only someone's belief.
Realis
The research word for "asserted": a specific event that actually happens in the story world.
One-value predicate
A fact that can only have one value at a time: where someone is, whether they are alive, who they are married to.
Two clocks
Reading order (the order of scenes in the book) and story time (the order things happen in the story). They differ in flashbacks.
Supersede
Mark an old claim as replaced by a newer extraction run, without deleting it.
Gold set
Answers written by hand, used to score every version of the pipeline.
F1, precision, recall
Precision: how much of what it found is right. Recall: how much of what is there it found. F1 combines both. 100 is perfect.
B³, LEA, CEAF, MUC
Standard scores for "did it group the mentions into the right characters". Each punishes different mistakes, so we report several.
Logprob
How sure the model is of each possible answer, read directly from it instead of trusting the word it prints.
Paired test
Run old and new on the same scenes and compare scene by scene. It sees small real differences that two separate runs would hide.

§1What the research says, and what to do with it

Four reviews covered the 14 topics you listed. Every number below was checked against the paper or repository itself. Two summaries the reviewers were given turned out to be invented, so they read the PDFs directly. Each idea gets a verdict: adopt (build it), test (build a measured experiment first), borrow the idea (use the concept, not the code), or skip.

1.1 Finding characters: small models link well, they do not find well

The single most useful result for us. Major Entity Identification (MEI) reframes coreference: instead of "group every mention in the book", it asks "here is the cast, which of them does this mention refer to?". On that task a 7–8B model is reasonable when the mentions are given, and nearly useless when it must find them itself.

1.3 Relations and events: a closed list, a quote, and "did it really happen?"

Free-text relation labels are the worst option: zero-shot document-level relation extraction scores under 20 F1 even for 70B models, and mapping free labels onto a list afterwards only roughly doubled a near-zero score. Fiction adds a second trap: much of what a scene says did not happen. "If he dies", "she imagined him dead", "he planned to leave" must never change the story's facts.

1.7 Checking consistency, and how hard it is

The sobering numbers
  • NoCha (EMNLP 2024): true/false claim pairs about 67 recent novels. GPT-4o 55.8%; every open-weight model scored below chance.
  • FlawedFictions (COLM 2025): finding plot holes. Llama-3.1-8B scored 0.10; always answering "no error" scored 0.50.
  • ConStory (2026): a 4-stage checker with exact quotes reached F1 0.678 with o4-mini, on injected errors, which are easier than natural ones (our own real-scene test showed the same gap).
  • FABLES (COLM 2024): the best automatic judge, given the whole book, found only 58.2 F1 of unfaithful claims.

The lesson for an 8B model: never ask it to check a whole book. Retrieve the two or three claims that could clash and check that small pair locally, with the quotes, the way ConStory's second stage does.

§2What exists today (short)

Unchanged from v1, which has the full picture. In one breath: one qwen3:8b JSON call per scene returns named people, places, up to 5 facts and up to 5 relations; import merges names with rules and a 60% logprob vote, while generated scenes match names exactly; relations are folded into about 16 types by regex and dated by chapter; entity states are regex over the facts; volumes are already a filter on one global network; the server copy of an edge drops its dates. About one minute per scene (Ethan Frome 10.8 minutes).

Measured against the research, the weak points are clear: mentions (exact names find fewer than 1 in 5), merging (a bare yes/no vote, uncalibrated), relations (free labels folded by regex), modality (nothing stops "if he dies" from killing him), and time (one clock, chapter-level).

§3The recommended architecture

How a scene flows through it, in plain steps:

  1. Text layer. The scene is split into sentences with character offsets, and gets a content hash and its two positions (reading order, optional story time).
  2. Who. Code proposes mentions: known names and aliases, capitalised names, "the + role" phrases ("the doctor"), and later GLiNER spans if they earn it. The model links each one to a cast id or NONE by logprob. New names go through the merge step: rules, then 5 candidates, then one listwise choice asked twice in shuffled order.
  3. What. One reader call per scene returns claims. Each has the quote first, then a predicate from the closed list, a modality and a visibility. Code checks every quote against the text and stores the offsets, swaps names for entity ids, applies the inverse/exclusion table, and maps unknown predicates (or parks them as unmapped).
  4. When. Non-asserted claims go on a branch. One-value predicates are updated by code. Other relations get candidates and one small labelling call, applied by code. A clash with no change shown on the page becomes a flag for the writer. The state timeline is derived from the claims.
  5. Use. Volume, chapter, scene and "as of scene N" views; summaries on the book's own tree; hybrid retrieval over text, summaries and timeline, never past scene N; the network panel, the writer's context and the continuity checks read from here.

§5Locked decisions

  • D1The model reads and chooses; code decides. Proposing candidates, checking quotes, applying rules and dating claims are code.
  • D2Find the cast, then link mentions to it. No end-to-end coreference by the 8B model.
  • D3Merging is a listwise choice, asked twice in shuffled order, with rules in the prompt and a cut-off tuned on the gold set.
  • D4Every claim has a quote with offsets and a modality. Only asserted claims change states.
  • D5Relations come from a closed list (Conan families plus our additions), with an inverse/exclusion table in code.
  • D6Two clocks, two histories: reading order and optional story time for validity; extraction run for supersession.
  • D7One-value predicates are updated by code. The model only labels duplicate/contradicts for the rest.
  • D8Volumes, chapters and timelines are views over claims that store only their scene.
  • D9Retrieval keeps the verbatim text next to summaries and the timeline, and never looks past scene N.
  • D10No Python server in the core. BookNLP and GLiNER are optional, and enter only through a measured gate.
  • D11Every step has a gate, scored on classics and on an unseen generated book, with paired tests.

§6How we will measure

This is the evaluation protocol every gate below uses. Thresholds are our proposals, not numbers from the literature; they can be moved once the first baseline is in.

LayerWhat is labelledMain metricsProposed bar
A · cast and mentionsWhole book: every character, place and object mentioned at least twice, its aliases, and every named or described mention (pronouns later)B³, CEAFe, LEA (MUC for comparison); pairwise merge precision and recall separately; mention-link precision/recallMerge precision ≥ 0.95 (a wrong merge is worse than a missed one); B³ ≥ 0.85 on named characters

C · time and statesFor each state (location, alive, married, knows): the scene where it starts and ends, and flashback scenes' story positionsStart/end within ±1 scene; state accuracy per scene; flag precision≥ 0.80 within ±1 scene
D · minimal pairsAbout 100 true/false claim pairs per book about states, relations and knowledge, tagged by scope (sentence, passage, whole book)Pair accuracy (both right)Above the baseline with the 95% interval of the difference excluding zero
E · retrievalAbout 40 questions per book in NovelQA style (times, relations, "as of scene N"), each with its evidence scenesRecall@5 of evidence scenes; answer accuracyRecall@5 ≥ plain embedding search, never worse
  • Books. Ethan Frome and The Time Machine (already imported), one of Versatile's own generated books (no model has memorised it), and BOOKCOREF's gold Pride and Prejudice for character linking at full-book scale.
  • Agreement. 20% of scenes labelled twice, at least a week apart. Report Cohen's κ for claims and B³ between the two passes. LitBank's annotators reached B³ about 94; nothing should be expected above our own agreement.
  • Statistics. Resample by scene (facts in one scene are not independent): 10,000-sample cluster bootstrap for intervals, Wilson intervals for single proportions, paired sign-flip permutation (100,000 random flips) for A/B. With 30 scenes, expect to see only differences of about 5 F1 or more; estimate the real number from the first pilot before each experiment.
  • Judges. An LLM judge may score lenient matches only after it agrees with hand labels on 50 items at κ ≥ 0.7.
  • Ship rule. A change ships only if its paired gain has a 95% interval above zero and no precision drops by more than 2 points.

§7Implementation roadmap

Twelve build steps and nine validation steps. A validation step is a real step: it has its own work, its own report, and it can send the plan back. The order comes from the research: measure first (everything else needs a number to beat), then the record shape (every later step writes claims), then who before what (claims need entity ids), then time (needs claims), then growth (needs time), then use.

  1. R0buildEvaluation foundation: gold set, scorers, harness#1095–7 days, mostly labelling
    What
    Label layers A–D of §6 for the two imported books and one generated book. Port MUC, B³, CEAFe and LEA to TypeScript. Write a harness that runs any pipeline version on the gold scenes and prints every metric with intervals.
    Why (hypothesis)
    Hypothesis: today's pipeline has measurable weak spots (mention recall, merge precision, modality) that the research predicts. Without a baseline nothing later can be called better.
    Inputs → outputs
    In: the three books, a labelling guide. Out: gold files (JSON) in the repo's test data, a scorer module, a harness command, a baseline report.
    Components
    TypeScript, Vitest; INCEpTION or a small in-app labelling screen; coval (Python) only to cross-check the port.
    Automated tests
    Scorer unit tests on hand-made cases with known answers; the TypeScript scores equal coval's on 3 files to 2 decimals.
    Check by hand
    Read 10 labelled scenes again after a week; time the first 5 scenes to re-plan the effort.
    Metrics
    All §6 metrics on the current pipeline, with 95% intervals.
    Success means
    A baseline report exists for all three books; the scorer matches coval; labelling time per scene is known.
    What can go wrong
    Labelling takes far longer than estimated; the guide is ambiguous (agreement low); the generated book is too easy or too short.
    Before moving on
    Gold files committed; baseline numbers recorded; V0 can start.

  8. R4buildMention candidates and linking (MEI)#1164–5 days
    What

A parallel experiment adds GLiNER (browser, ONNX) as a second candidate source.
    Why (hypothesis)
    Hypothesis: proposed mentions plus model linking recover most of the 4-in-5 mentions that names alone miss, at ≥ 0.90 precision.
    Inputs → outputs
    In: scene sentences, cast registry. Out: mention → entity links with probabilities; presence per scene.
    Components
    TypeScript candidate finder; qwen3:8b logprob choice; optional gliner npm package.
    Automated tests

    Check by hand
    Read 50 low-confidence links and 50 NONE decisions.
    Metrics
    Mention-link precision and recall vs layer A; recall gain over exact names; seconds per scene.
    Success means
    Precision ≥ 0.90; recall gain ≥ +20 points over exact names; import time within +20%.

  10. R5buildClaim extraction v2#1184–5 days + live runs
    What
    New reader prompt and JSON schema: for each claim, the quote first, then subject, predicate from the closed list (Conan families + our state predicates + told), object or value, modality and visibility.     Why (hypothesis)
    Hypothesis: a closed list, quotes first and modality raise claim precision to ≥ 0.90 and make hypotheticals stop changing facts.
    Inputs → outputs
    In: scene text, linked mentions. Out: claims with quotes, offsets and modality; a rejected-claims log.
    Components
    qwen3:8b via Ollama structured output; the predicate list (JSON with definitions); quote checker with offsets.
    Automated tests
    Schema tests; quote checker on tricky text (curly quotes, ellipses); modality tests ("if he dies", "she imagined", "he planned").
    Check by hand
    Read 30 scenes' claims side by side with the text.
    Metrics
    Strict and lenient precision/recall vs layer B; grounding rate; modality accuracy; time per scene.
    Success means
    Precision ≥ 0.90; lenient recall ≥ 0.60; grounded ≥ 0.95; time within +20%.
    What can go wrong
    Longer answers time out; the model copies quotes loosely; the closed list forces wrong labels instead of unmapped.
    Before moving on
    R6 can start; A/B results recorded.

§8Open questions and backlog

Open

  • The relation list. Conan's 5 families are the base. Which finer types do we keep for a novel-writing app, and which state predicates (location, alive, married_to, holds_title, owns, knows) do we mark as one-value?
  • Story time. Does the writer set flashback positions by hand, or do we ask the model and let the writer correct it?
  • Who labels. You, or the reviewer setup used for the 78-scene judge labels? Labelling is the largest cost of R0.
  • Optional downloads. Is a 258 MB GLiNER model acceptable for users, if it passes V3?

Backlog

  • Everything in R12.
  • A merge-and-split screen for entities.
  • Per-genre threshold calibration once more books are labelled.
